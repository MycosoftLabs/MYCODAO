## Mycelium as a material

Mycelium, the thread-like body of a fungus, binds whatever it grows through. Grown on agricultural waste such as hemp hurd or sawdust, it forms light, compostable **composites** used for packaging, acoustic panels and insulation.

## Leather-like sheets

Dense mats of mycelium can be grown, treated and finished into **leather alternatives**. Strain choice and growth conditions decide thickness, strength and texture, so documented cultures are valuable.

## Chitin and chitosan

Fungal cell walls contain **chitin**, which can be converted into **chitosan**, used in wound dressings, water treatment and biodegradable films.

## Food

**Mycoprotein** from *Fusarium venenatum* has been sold as food in the UK since 1985. Fermentation of fungal biomass is now a major area of alternative-protein research.

## Why this matters for the cycle

Each material starts with a specific strain. A biobank of well-documented cultures, with provenance and permissions, is what lets a materials company license the right strain, and what lets payment flow back to discoverers.

## A short history of fungal medicine

- **Penicillin** (*Penicillium*) — observed by Alexander Fleming in 1928 and mass-produced in the 1940s. It launched the antibiotic era.
- **Cyclosporine** (*Tolypocladium inflatum*) — an immunosuppressant, approved in the US in 1983, that made routine organ transplants possible.
- **Lovastatin** (*Aspergillus terreus*) — the first statin approved by the FDA, in 1987, for lowering cholesterol.
- **Griseofulvin** (*Penicillium griseofulvum*) — an oral antifungal for skin and nail infections.
- **Echinocandins** — antifungals such as caspofungin, derived from compounds made by *Glarea lozoyensis*.
- **PSK** (*Trametes versicolor*, turkey tail) — a polysaccharide used alongside cancer treatment in Japan.
- **Psilocybin** (*Psilocybe* species) — under clinical study for depression; the FDA has granted it Breakthrough Therapy designation. It remains a controlled substance in the US.

## Where new medicines come from

Most fungi have never been screened. Each new culture is a library of possible compounds: antibiotics, antifungals, anticancer agents, enzymes and immunomodulators. Screening new isolates is how the next penicillin is found.

## What this means for MycoDAO

Discovery, culture and screening are the early, high-value steps, and they are exactly what the biobank and species rewards support. Development beyond that needs pharmaceutical partners, clinical trials and regulators.

## Poisons worth understanding

- **Amatoxins** (*Amanita phalloides*, death cap, and relatives) block RNA polymerase II and cause most fatal mushroom poisonings. Alpha-amanitin is also a research tool, and it is being studied as a payload in targeted cancer drugs.
- **Ergot alkaloids** (*Claviceps purpurea*) caused historic ergotism outbreaks. Derivatives became medicines: ergotamine for migraine and ergometrine for postpartum bleeding. LSD was first synthesized from lysergic acid in 1938.
- **Aflatoxins** (*Aspergillus flavus*) contaminate stored grain and nuts and are potent carcinogens. Food safety programs monitor them worldwide.
- **Orellanine** (some *Cortinarius* species) causes delayed kidney failure.

## Why toxins belong in the economy

Toxins drive the need for accurate identification, food-safety testing and antidote research, and some become drugs or research reagents. A well-documented toxic culture is as scientifically valuable as an edible one.

## Safety

- Never eat a wild mushroom without identification by a qualified expert.
- Toxic cultures are handled only by trained labs under the applicable rules.
- Blocks does not publish dosing, extraction or preparation instructions for toxic or controlled compounds.

## The pipeline

1. **Isolate and culture** a documented strain.
2. **Screen** extracts against targets (bacteria, fungi, cancer cell lines, enzymes).
3. **Extract and characterize** active compounds, identifying them by mass spectrometry and NMR.
4. **Preclinical studies** test safety and activity in the lab.
5. **Clinical trials** (Phase I–III) test safety and efficacy in people. Drug development commonly takes 10 years or more.
6. **Regulatory review** (FDA, EMA and others) decides approval.

Materials follow a shorter path: lab tests, pilot production, performance and safety certification, then manufacturing.

## Where value accrues

Value grows at each step, but so does cost and risk. Early steps — discovery, culture, screening data — are where a DAO-funded biobank adds the most. Later steps are done with partners under agreements that can include milestone payments or royalties *to the rights holder named in the agreement*.

## Closing the loop

When an agreement produces income, the treasury can direct part of it to rewards and bounties for new discovery. That is the circular economy: discovery funds products, and products fund discovery. The payment terms are planned and will be published per agreement.

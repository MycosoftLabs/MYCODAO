## The pace of discovery

In recent years about two thousand new fungal species have been formally described each year. That is still a tiny fraction of the estimated 2.2–3.8 million species. Many come from soils, insects, plant tissues, lichens, the deep sea and extreme environments such as Antarctic rock and hot deserts.

## How a species is named now

1. **Collect** a specimen or isolate a culture, with full provenance.
2. **Sequence** the **ITS region**, the official fungal DNA barcode, plus other markers such as LSU, TEF1 or RPB2. More and more often the whole genome is sequenced.
3. **Build a phylogeny** to show the new lineage is distinct from its relatives.
4. **Describe** its morphology: spores, hyphae and fruiting bodies.
5. **Publish** with a type specimen deposited in a collection and a registered name (MycoBank, Index Fungorum). Papers mark the new name as *sp. nov.* (new species) or *gen. nov.* (new genus).

## Why it matters for MycoDAO

Every new species is a new genome, and potentially new chemistry. Paying foragers, documenting provenance and culturing tissue all speed up this pipeline. Open the Research tab's **New species** topic to see the latest descriptions as they are published.

## Secondary metabolites

Fungi make **secondary metabolites**: molecules not needed for basic growth, used for defense, communication and competition. They include antibiotics (penicillin), immunosuppressants (cyclosporine), statins (lovastatin), toxins (aflatoxin, amatoxin) and pigments.

## Biosynthetic gene clusters

The genes for one metabolite usually sit together in a **biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC)**. Main families:

- **Polyketide synthases (PKS)**: statins, aflatoxins
- **Non-ribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPS)**: penicillin, cyclosporine
- **Terpene synthases**: many aromas and bioactive terpenoids
- **RiPPs**: ribosomal peptides such as amanitin

## Silent clusters

A typical filamentous fungus carries dozens of BGCs, but most are "silent" under laboratory conditions. Researchers wake them by changing culture conditions, co-culturing with other microbes, editing regulators, or moving the cluster into a host such as *Aspergillus nidulans* or yeast.

## Genome mining

With thousands of fungal genomes now public, tools such as **antiSMASH** predict BGCs straight from DNA. Every new species sequenced adds new clusters, and so new candidate molecules. This is why biobank cultures with genomes attached are so valuable.

## Fungi have electrical activity

Mycelium shows spikes of electrical potential travelling along hyphae. In 2022 Andrew Adamatzky reported spike trains in four species and grouped them into patterns resembling a vocabulary of up to about 50 "words". What these patterns mean biologically is still open.

## Fungi controlling machines

- **Biohybrid robots (2024).** Cornell researchers used electrical signals from king oyster mycelium to drive the movement of two robots, responding to light (*Science Robotics*).
- **Mycelium skins for electronics (2022).** *Ganoderma* mycelium sheets were used as biodegradable substrates for circuits and batteries ("MycelioTronics", *Science Advances*).
- **Living fungal bioelectronics (2026).** Bioprinted living fungal sensors and mycelium actuators are now appearing in journals such as *PNAS* and *Nature Communications*. See the Research tab's **Computing** topic.

## Fungal computing

Unconventional computing studies whether mycelium networks can process signals, store state, or act as memristors. Mycosoft's **Fungal Compute Interface (FCI)** program explores interfaces between living mycelium and electronics; it is at the incubation stage.

## Mycorrhizal networks: what is known

Fungi link plant roots into **common mycorrhizal networks**. Field studies since 1997 have shown carbon and nutrients moving between plants through fungi. A 2023 review warned that popular claims (trees "talking" or mothers feeding seedlings) go beyond the evidence. Treat strong claims cautiously, and check the papers.

## The resistance problem

In 2022 the WHO published its first **fungal priority pathogens list**, led by *Candida auris*, *Aspergillus fumigatus*, *Cryptococcus neoformans* and *Candida albicans*. Only a few classes of antifungal drug exist (azoles, polyenes, echinocandins), and resistance is rising, partly driven by agricultural fungicides.

## Fungi as a source

- **Antibacterials:** penicillins and cephalosporins come from fungi, and fungal extracts are still screened for activity against drug-resistant bacteria.
- **Antifungals:** echinocandins (such as caspofungin) derive from a fungal metabolite. Fungi make antifungal compounds to compete with each other, so cultures that win on a shared plate are promising leads.
- **Antivirals:** extracts of several mushrooms and polypores show antiviral activity in laboratory and animal studies. Few have reached human trials, so treat these as research leads, not treatments.

## How tissue becomes a drug lead

1. Culture the fungus from documented tissue.
2. Grow it under varied conditions to switch on silent gene clusters.
3. Screen extracts against target microbes.
4. Isolate the active molecule and identify its structure.
5. Test toxicity and activity before any clinical step.

Biobank cultures with provenance and genomes make each of these steps faster and legally clean.

## Approved adjuncts

- **PSK** (from *Trametes versicolor*) and **lentinan** (from shiitake, *Lentinula edodes*) are polysaccharides used in Japan alongside chemotherapy for some cancers. Evidence for benefit varies by cancer type; neither replaces standard treatment.

## Toxins as targeted payloads

**Alpha-amanitin**, the death cap toxin, stops RNA polymerase II in any cell it enters. Linked to an antibody that seeks out tumor cells (an *antibody–drug conjugate*), it becomes a targeted payload. Amanitin conjugates are in early clinical trials for cancers such as multiple myeloma.

## Endophytes and anticancer chemistry

Fungi living inside plants (*endophytes*) make many bioactive compounds. A 1993 report that an endophyte of the Pacific yew made paclitaxel (Taxol) launched the field, though fungal production of paclitaxel has since been disputed. Endophytes remain a rich source of cytotoxic leads.

## Psilocybin and cancer-related distress

Two 2016 randomized trials (NYU and Johns Hopkins) found that a single psilocybin session with psychological support reduced depression and anxiety in people with life-threatening cancer, with effects lasting months. Psilocybin is a controlled substance; these were regulated clinical studies.

## Yeast: the aging laboratory

Baker's yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) is one of the main model organisms of aging research:

- **Replicative lifespan** — how many times a mother cell can bud — and **chronological lifespan** are measured in yeast.
- The **TOR** genes, targets of the drug rapamycin, were identified in yeast in 1991. (Rapamycin itself comes from a bacterium, not a fungus.)
- Yoshinori Ohsumi's 2016 Nobel Prize recognized his discovery of **autophagy** genes in yeast; autophagy is central to cellular aging.

## Mushroom molecules studied for healthy aging

- **Ergothioneine** — an antioxidant amino acid made by fungi and some bacteria but not by plants or animals. Humans have a dedicated transporter for it. Mushrooms are the richest dietary source, and it has been proposed as a "longevity vitamin."
- **Spermidine** — a polyamine found in mushrooms, wheat germ and soy. It induces autophagy and extends lifespan in yeast, worms, flies and mice. Human evidence is still limited.

## Where the field is going

Researchers are engineering yeast to produce ergothioneine at scale, and studying how fungal compounds affect autophagy and inflammation. Claims about human lifespan are not yet established.

## Editing fungal genomes

CRISPR-Cas9 was adapted to filamentous fungi such as *Aspergillus* in 2015 and now works in many species, including mushrooms. Researchers use it to:

- switch on silent gene clusters and discover new compounds
- delete toxin genes from production strains
- improve enzyme and protein yields
- study gene function in pathogens such as *Candida* and *Fusarium*

A CRISPR-edited white button mushroom that resists browning was ruled outside US biotech regulation in 2016, because it carried no foreign DNA.

## Yeast as a chemical factory

Metabolic engineering rebuilds whole biosynthetic pathways inside yeast:

- **Artemisinic acid** (the antimalarial precursor), 2013
- **Opioids**, made entirely in yeast from sugar, 2015
- **Psilocybin** and related tryptamines, 2020
- Cannabinoids, flavors, fragrances and many other compounds

## Why it matters

Synthetic biology turns a discovery into a scalable process: once a gene cluster is known, it can be moved into a production host. That is why sequencing biobank cultures matters. The genome is the blueprint for the product.

## Responsibility

Engineering pathogens or toxin pathways is tightly regulated. MycoDAO only supports work done under the applicable biosafety rules and institutional oversight.

## Surviving space

- **Extreme survivors.** The black Antarctic fungus *Cryomyces antarcticus* survived 18 months outside the International Space Station in an exposure experiment.
- **Radiotrophic fungi.** Melanized fungi found at Chernobyl grow better under ionizing radiation; melanin changes its electronic properties when irradiated. A 2018–2019 ISS experiment suggested a layer of *Cladosporium sphaerospermum* attenuated some radiation, which has led to proposals for living radiation shields.

## Growing habitats

NASA-funded **myco-architecture** research (led by Lynn Rothschild at NASA Ames) studies sending dormant fungi to grow building materials on site, using mycelium as a self-assembling, self-repairing structure.

## The Martian soil problem

Martian regolith is not soil. It lacks organic matter, and the Phoenix lander (2008) found roughly 0.5% **perchlorate**, a chlorine-oxygen salt that is toxic to humans and interferes with thyroid function.

- **Removing perchlorate:** the best-studied biological route is *perchlorate-reducing bacteria*, which break perchlorate down to chloride and oxygen. Washing and chemical methods are also studied.
- **Building soil:** fungi are being studied for the next step. Mycelium binds particles into aggregates, decomposes organic waste into humus, weathers minerals, and mycorrhizal fungi help plants take up nutrients in regolith simulants.
- **Fungal perchlorate tolerance** and fungal–bacterial consortia are an active research question, not a demonstrated technology.

A plausible Mars pipeline: bacteria remove perchlorate, fungi turn crew and crop waste into organic matter, and mycorrhizae help crops grow. This combination is a research direction, not yet a proven system.

## Eating plastic

In 2011 Yale students found an Amazonian endophyte, *Pestalotiopsis microspora*, that can degrade polyurethane, even without oxygen. Since then, fungi that partly break down polyethylene, polystyrene and other plastics have been reported. Speeds are still slow compared with how much plastic is produced, so this is research, not a solution yet.

## Cleaning pollution (mycoremediation)

White-rot fungi produce powerful enzymes (laccases and peroxidases) that break down lignin, and also many pollutants: petroleum hydrocarbons, dyes and some pesticides. Fungi can also take up and concentrate heavy metals. The metals do not disappear: they must be harvested and disposed of safely, which is why accurate wording matters in projects such as Project Oyster.

## Living with radiation

Melanized fungi grow toward radiation sources at Chernobyl and are being studied as living radiation shields for spacecraft.

## Living materials

Researchers are building materials that stay alive: mycelium composites that self-repair, sense their environment, or grow into shape. Living mycelium actuators and sensors are among the newest results.

## Treat with care

Frontier results are often single studies. Look for replication, sample sizes and peer review before treating a claim as established.

## Landmarks

- **Hy-Fi (2014):** a tower of mycelium bricks at MoMA PS1 in New York showed compostable construction at architectural scale.
- **MycelioTronics (2022):** mycelium skins used as biodegradable bases for circuits and batteries.

## What decides performance

Strain, substrate, growth conditions and post-processing (pressing, heat, tanning) set strength, density and water resistance. That is why strain libraries matter: the right culture is often the difference between a lab sample and a product.

## The economic loop

Materials companies need reliable, documented strains. A biobank that supplies them under clear terms, and pays discoverers when a strain becomes a product, is the circular economy that MycoDAO is building.
